## Source synopsis

The thread opens with a verification question: does bronze contain copper. Copilot confirmed that copper is the base and dominant metal, giving the standard formula as roughly 88 to 90 percent copper with tin at around 10 to 12 percent, and noting that other metals including aluminum, zinc, manganese, and nickel can substitute for or supplement tin to adjust hardness, strength, and corrosion resistance. It added that early bronzes sometimes used arsenic instead of tin, which gave strength but was toxic to refine. The functional reasoning it supplied: pure copper is relatively soft, so alloying hardens it; bronze melts more easily than pure copper, which makes it castable for tools, weapons, and statues; and bronze resists corrosion better than iron, which is why it dominated before the Iron Age. Sources cited were Wikipedia, Britannica, and Science Notes and Projects.

The second user turn asked the question that actually matters for rendering: would aged bronze have heavy greenish oxidation and scaling built up over hundreds or thousands of years. The answer corrected the premise. Bronze does develop a greenish patina over centuries, but it is normally a thin protective layer rather than heavy scaling. The mechanism given: exposure to air, moisture, and pollutants drives reactions that form copper oxides, copper carbonates, and copper chlorides. Two of the carbonates were named as minerals, malachite and azurite, and these are identified as the source of the green and blue hues. Unlike rust on iron, the patina is described as stable and protective, bonding to the surface and slowing further corrosion.

Copilot then drew an explicit distinction between patina and scaling. Patina is a thin, even coating that bonds and stabilizes. Environment shifts both the composition and the appearance: marine environments introduce copper chlorides, which may present as more powdery or flaky; urban environments introduce sulfates, which push the tone bluish; and over millennia in harsh conditions such as saltwater or acidic soil, bronze can develop thicker uneven corrosion layers that look like scaling but which conservators distinguish from desirable patina. Historical examples given: Greek and Roman bronzes showing smooth green patinas after more than two thousand years; archaeological finds buried in soil showing thicker encrustations mixed with mineral deposits; and outdoor monuments exposed to rain and pollution patinating faster, often within decades. The Statue of Liberty was cited as the well-known copper-patina reference.

The turn closed with a preservation note that doubles as a failure-mode description. Bronze disease occurs in humid or salty environments when unstable copper chlorides form, producing a powdery green corrosion that eats into the metal. It is destructive and requires conservation treatment, and it is explicitly contrasted with the stable protective patina that collectors and conservators preserve rather than remove. Sources cited were walkermetalsmith.com, europeanbronze.com, and Impact Signs.

The third user turn abandoned research and supplied a single consolidated MurderBird prompt, collapsing the entire correction chain from the sibling thread into one paragraph: the blueprint-infused steampunk terror-bird construction, the more-metallic and less-organic feather instruction, the Bronze Age to 1870s to 2025 fabrication history with the cybernetic brain, the thousands of years of patina and greening, and the penny-at-the-bottom-of-a-wishing-well color target in darkened greens, blues, and greys. Copilot reported a render and described the feathers as reworked to look more metallic and engineered, layered with centuries of verdigris greens, blues, and greys, with the glowing cybernetic eye cutting through the aged texture. That render was never supplied as a file.

## Decisions and rationale

1. **Treat the palette as copper chemistry, not color preference.** Bronze is 88 to 90 percent copper, so the aged surface is governed by copper corrosion products. This is the reframing that makes the whole spec falsifiable: a proposed color is either consistent with a named compound and environment or it is not.

2. **Accept the thin-patina correction and render accordingly.** The user's stated expectation was heavy oxidation and scaling. The source rejected it: patina is normally a thin, even, bonded coating. For rendering this means the plate geometry must stay legible under the finish. Rivets, hammer facets, plate edges, and machined detail should read through the patina, not be buried by it. A bird that looks like it was dipped in green crust has lost the fabrication history that is the whole point of the three-epoch canon.

3. **Choose still fresh-water submersion as the governing environment.** The sigil's terminal target is a penny at the bottom of a wishing well for a hundred years. Mapped onto this thread's environment table, that is submerged, low-oxygen, low-chloride, mineral-present. It produces darkened green and blue carbonate tones with grey-black mineral staining, not the bright chalky turquoise of an air-exposed monument. This is the reconciliation between the two threads and it resolves the ambiguity in the word verdigris, which the assistant used loosely to mean both.

4. **Exclude bronze disease from the render.** Powdery, flaking, actively destructive corrosion is a distinct and undesirable state. The MurderBird is a functioning artifact that was recovered and rebuilt, not a specimen decaying in a crate. Stable patina reads as survival. Bronze disease reads as failure.

5. **Keep blue in the palette as a physical claim.** Azurite is a copper carbonate and is the named source of blue. The blue in the sigil spec is therefore not a stylistic flourish and should not be dropped as an outlier when a generator drifts toward monochrome green.

### Render rules derived from the material model

1. Patina is a skin, not a layer of sediment. Plate edges, rivet heads, hammer facets, and machined detail read through it.
2. High points wear back toward metal. Beak ridge, talon tips, leading feather edges, and any surface that would be handled or struck show exposed alloy. Wear is where the object's use history lives.
3. Recesses hold the darkest and greenest deposit. Between plates, under overlaps, behind rivet heads.
4. Green and blue are the same chemistry at different ratios. They should interleave, not sit in separate zones.
5. Grey and black are mineral staining and oxide, not desaturated green. They supply the value range the spec calls for.
6. No powder, no flake, no scale. Those signal chloride corrosion and active decay.
7. Differential aging by metal. Original bronze goes green and blue. Any 1870s steel goes black and rust. Any 1870s brass goes brown-gold. The 2025 insert has not aged.
8. Contrast is lifted through value separation between plate faces, not through overall brightness. The spec asks for brightness that does not lose industrial depth, and value separation is how those coexist.
